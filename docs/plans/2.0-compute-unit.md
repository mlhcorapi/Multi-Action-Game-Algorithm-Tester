# MAGAT 2.0: a compute unit from a basket of operations

**Status: a plan, 2026-11-14.** Nothing here is implemented. MAGAT 1.0 stays as released. Whether 2.0
changes its unit is decided by the measurements below, by a rule written down before they are taken
([section 8](#8-the-decision-rule)).

## 1. Why change the unit

In 1.0, one compute unit is one forward-model `advance` by `emcts` at 9,500 units, on the machine the
currency was measured on ([`CURRENCY.md`](../CURRENCY.md#the-unit)). That has three weaknesses:
- **It belongs to one algorithm.** A change to `emcts`'s code, or to what its turns reach, moves every
  currency's unit.
- **One operation stands for the whole machine.** Whatever is peculiar about that operation on a given
  processor becomes the unit's peculiarity. On the laptop, `emcts`'s work is about 1% cheaper relative to
  the unit than on the R7615, while `oep`'s and `tbets`'s is 7-9% dearer
  ([`paper/tables/prices.md`](../../paper/tables/prices.md)).
- **It means nothing outside MAGAT.** A unit defined by a game-playing algorithm cannot be a measure of
  compute for anything else.

A **basket** defines the unit as the time of a fixed set of operations, combined: a price index rather
than the price of one good. This plan finds the best basket, and considers one that involves no
decision-making algorithm at all, so the unit could serve as a proxy for time in other settings.

## 2. What a basket can and cannot do

A currency prices each arm's work on one machine. With `u` the unit's nanoseconds on machine `M`, and
`c(p, M)` the nanoseconds of profile `p`'s operation mix there, a budget of `B` units buys profile `p`
the work `W(p, M) = B · u(M) / c(p, M)`. Between machines `i` and `j`, the error in what a level means
for `p` is:

```
d(p; i, j) = log W(p, i) − log W(p, j) = log(u(i) / u(j)) − log(c(p, i) / c(p, j))
```

**The basket chooses only the first term: one number per pair of machines.** Every profile's error
moves by the same amount, so the spread between profiles is fixed by the hardware, whatever the basket.
The best any unit can do is centre the errors on zero. The spread that is left is the floor.

The pilot, from the two published currencies (`compute_operations.csv` of `r7615-time` and
`laptop-i9-14900hx-time`, 12 profiles):

| Unit | Typical error (RMS) | Worst | Share of the reducible error removed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0: one `emcts@9500` advance | 5.7% | 8.5% | 0% |
| geometric mean of the 12 profiles' advances | 4.0% | 5.0% | ~100% |
| geometric mean of the 12 profiles' whole operation mixes | 4.0% | 5.3% | ~100% |
| the floor (no unit can go below it) | 4.0% | - | - |

So on these two machines a basket would cut the worst error by about 40% and the typical one by about
30%, all the way to the floor. It would not remove the floor's ~4%: that is a real difference in how
the two processors run each algorithm. Two machines are not enough to choose a basket, but they set
expectations. **The aim is a unit whose error is at the floor on machines it was not chosen on, and
which stays there when algorithms are added.**

## 3. What "best" means

Each candidate basket is scored on:
1. **Cross-machine error**, the primary measure: the RMS of `d(p; i, j)` over profiles and pairs of
   machines, computed only on **held-out** data (section 7). The worst case is reported beside it.
2. **The share of the reducible error removed:** `(E_1.0 − E_basket) / (E_1.0 − floor)`, so baskets
   compare across sets of machines whose floors differ.
3. **Stability:** the unit's own spread across repeat calibrations of one machine. A unit must be
   measurable to within 1%, or its noise swamps what it fixes.
4. **Independence:** whether the unit changes when an algorithm is added, changed or removed.
5. **Reach:** for the decision-free baskets, how well the unit predicts the relative time of workloads
   outside MAGAT across the same machines (section 4.3).
6. **Cost and clarity:** a few minutes to measure, and one sentence to explain.

## 4. The candidate baskets

| | Basket | Built from | Depends on the algorithms? |
|---|---|---|---|
| B0 | **1.0's unit** | one `emcts@9500` advance | yes |
| B1 | **the algorithms' advances** | the geometric mean of every profile's advance price | yes |
| B2 | **the algorithms' work** | the geometric mean of every profile's whole priced mix | yes |
| B3 | **the game's operations** | `advance`, `copy` and `evaluate` on a fixed set of portfolio states, outside any algorithm | the game, not the algorithms |
| B4 | **generic kernels** | a fixed suite of small programs that involve no game and no algorithm (4.2) | neither |
| B5 | **game and generic** | B3 and B4 together | the game, not the algorithms |

**How they combine:** every basket is a **geometric mean** of its parts' times. An arithmetic mix, such
as "10% of the time of each of 10 operations", lets the slowest part dominate. A geometric mean gives
every part's relative speed the same say, and is the standard way to combine benchmark ratios. Its parts
are equally weighted by default. Weights fitted to minimise the error are tried too, but only under
cross-validation (section 7), because with a handful of machines they overfit.

### 4.1 The game's operations (B3)

B3 is the forward model exercised directly: on 64 fixed portfolio positions, `advance` along a fixed
sequence of legal actions, then `copy` and `evaluate` the states reached, single-threaded, with fixed
seeds, timed per operation. It is what every algorithm spends most of its budget on, but it is measured
outside any algorithm. Adding or changing an algorithm then cannot move the unit. It is MAGAT's game
interface, so every game MAGAT adds gets its own B3 for free.

### 4.2 Generic kernels (B4): a unit for compute in general

B4 is meant to work as a proxy for single-threaded CPU time well beyond decision-making. Each kernel is
small, deterministic, written once in Java, and stresses a different way processors differ:

| Kernel | What it does | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| `int-hash` | hashes a fixed integer stream | the integer units, few memory accesses |
| `branchy` | runs a fixed state machine on a pseudo-random input | branch prediction |
| `pointer-chase-l2` | follows a random cycle through a 256 KB array | L2 latency |
| `pointer-chase-dram` | the same through 256 MB | memory latency |
| `stream` | sums and copies large arrays | memory bandwidth |
| `alloc` | builds and drops many small objects | allocation and garbage collection |
| `hashmap` | inserts, looks up and removes in a hash map | mixed: hashing, cache misses, allocation |
| `sort` | sorts a fixed array of records | comparisons, branches, moves |
| `float` | dot products and a small matrix multiply | floating point |
| `string` | builds, splits and parses text | allocation, copying |

Each kernel's work is fixed: fixed sizes, fixed seeds, and a result checked against a known value, so
the JIT cannot skip it. It is timed as the calibrator times operations: a warm-up, then the fastest of
several repetitions, single-threaded, on the same pinned cores as the calibration. The whole suite takes
a minute or two. Its version (`magat-basket-1`) is part of the unit's definition, so a change to a kernel
is a new basket, never a silent change.

### 4.3 Testing B4 beyond MAGAT

To claim the unit is a proxy for time outside decision-making, it is tested on workloads it was not built
from. On every machine of the study, time a handful of CPU-bound programs unrelated to the kernels:
compressing a fixed file, a constraint solver on fixed instances, another game's forward model, and a
compiler build of a fixed source tree. The question is whether the basket predicts their relative times
across machines better than any single kernel, or a single `advance`, does. This is an honest test of
reach. A unit for compute in general would also make MAGAT's budgets meaningful to people outside game
AI, but that claim needs this evidence before the paper makes it.

## 5. Machines

The basket is chosen to work across processors, so the study needs processors that differ. In order of
value:

| Machine | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|
| R7615 (AMD EPYC 9534, Zen 4, Linux container) | the reference machine | measured once |
| Laptop (Intel i9-14900HX, Raptor Lake hybrid, Windows) | a consumer processor with performance and efficient cores | measured once |
| An ARM machine (Apple M-series, macOS) | a different instruction set and memory system: the largest expected differences | needed |
| An older Intel desktop or server (another cache layout) | a third x86 design | needed |
| A cloud virtual machine | shared tenancy, as many users will run it | needed |
| More, if available | each machine adds pairs; six machines give 15 | useful |

Plus **two more calibrations each on the R7615 and the laptop, on different days**. Their spread is the
noise floor: a difference between baskets smaller than it is not a difference.

## 6. What each machine measures

1. **`calibrate-controlled`**, as in 1.0: every reference algorithm at the published levels, which gives
   `c(p, M)` for every profile. Several hours.
2. **The basket suite** (B3 and B4), in the same session: measured before and after the calibration, as
   the reference cell is bracketed now, so a machine that changed speed mid-session is caught. Minutes.
3. **The outside workloads** of section 4.3, on as many machines as practical. Minutes to an hour.
4. On the laptop and one other machine, **the reference experiment again** under the winning unit, for the
   outcome check (section 7). About an hour and a half on the laptop.

Each machine's results travel as a package: `magat export --currency`, plus the basket's and the outside
workloads' timings in the calibration record ([`SHARING.md`](../SHARING.md)).

## 7. The analysis

A tool, `tools/unit_study.py`, which generalises the pilot, reads every machine's package and reports for
each basket:
- the error matrix `d(p; i, j)`, its RMS and worst case, the floor, and the share removed;
- **leave one algorithm out:** the basket is built without one algorithm's operations and scored on it,
  so it is judged on algorithms it was not made from, as every future algorithm will be;
- **leave one machine out:** fitted weights are fitted without a machine and scored on it;
- intervals by resampling machines and repeat sessions;
- the unit's stability across repeat sessions;
- for B4, its prediction of the outside workloads' relative times.

**The outcome check.** A unit is one number per machine, so a run under a new unit is a run of 1.0's at
rescaled levels. On the laptop, the reference experiment is run at the levels the winning unit implies
(for the geometric mean of the mixes, about 4.5% more than now), then compared with the R7615 run
(`analyze --compare`). The prediction, from section 2: `oep`'s and `tbets`'s agreement with the R7615
improves, `emcts`'s falls slightly, and the ranking agrees as before. If the turns do not move the way
the prices say, the price model is missing something, which matters more than the basket.

## 8. The decision rule

Written before any measurement. 2.0 adopts basket X if all of these hold:
1. **It beats 1.0's unit on held-out data:** its RMS error, on held-out algorithms and machines, is
   below B0's by more than twice the noise floor, with a resampling interval that excludes zero.
2. **It is stable:** its unit varies by at most 1% across repeat sessions on one machine.
3. **It is the most independent among the ones that tie:** a decision-free basket (B3, B4, B5) is
   preferred to an algorithm-built one (B1, B2) whenever their errors are within the noise floor of
   each other, and B4 or B5 is preferred to B3 when they also tie on the outside workloads.

If no basket passes, 2.0 keeps 1.0's unit. The study is then a result in its own right: the error the
unit leaves is the hardware's, and no definition of the unit removes it.

## 9. How 2.0 would carry a new unit

- **Currency format 3.** The header names the basket and records its time and every part's time:
  `unit = magat-basket-1`, `unitNanos = ...`, with each kernel's time in nanoseconds. Prices are ratios to
  the basket's time. Format 2 files still read. Every result records its currency's hash, and reuse and
  import already compare prices leaf by leaf, so a 1.0 result is never mixed with a 2.0 one.
- **The R7615 as the base.** The basket unit is scaled once, by a constant fixed forever, so that it equals
  1.0's unit on the R7615. The R7615's prices, its levels (9,500 / 20,000 / 101,500 units = 500 / 1000 /
  5000 ms there) and its published run are then unchanged and still reusable. Only other machines' units
  move, and they re-calibrate for 2.0 in any case.
- **A shared price table, as a separate option.** A basket brings every machine's unit closer to the same
  meaning; only one table of prices used everywhere makes turns identical everywhere. That is a choice
  between "a share of my machine's time" and "the reference machine's work", and is worth offering beside
  the new unit, not instead of it.

## 10. The work, as pull requests

1. **`tools/unit_study.py` and the two-machine pilot report**, from the published currencies alone. It
   changes nothing in MAGAT.
2. **`magat basket`:** the B3 and B4 suites, deterministic and tested, measured by `calibrate-controlled` and
   kept in its record. The currency is not changed yet.
3. **Measuring the machines** (yours): calibrations, the basket, the repeats and the outside workloads,
   exported as packages.
4. **The analysis and the decision:** `docs/results/compute-unit.md`, reproducible from the packages, with
   the rule of section 8 applied.
5. **If a basket is adopted:** currency format 3, the R7615 base, the docs and migration notes.
6. **The outcome check** on the laptop.
7. **2.0, with the paper.**

## 11. Risks

| Risk | What guards against it |
|---|---|
| Too few machines to choose between baskets | repeat sessions for the noise floor, leave-one-out, and a decision rule that keeps 1.0's unit when the evidence is thin |
| Fitted weights that fit these machines only | equal weights by default; fitted ones only under leave-one-machine-out |
| The JIT removing a kernel's work | every kernel's result is checked against a known value |
| A machine changing speed during the session | the basket measured before and after, as the reference cell is now |
| Kernels that miss how an algorithm uses memory | that is the floor of section 2: measured and reported, not hidden |
| A unit for "compute in general" that is not | section 4.3 tests it on unrelated workloads before the paper claims it |
